# Creative Direction Reference

Use this guide to turn a broad brief into a strong visual premise.

## Build a visual premise

A useful concept can be summarized as:

**Subject + unexpected context + visual tension + emotional meaning**

For example, instead of "a person in a futuristic garden", consider a portrait subject standing inside a greenhouse where the plants grow in precise geometric patterns around a suspended rain cloud. The unusual element should reveal mood or story, not merely decorate the scene.

## Creative combination matrix

Combine two or three compatible domains:

- Natural systems: roots, coral, fungi, seed pods, erosion, weather, migration.
- Built environments: infrastructure, archives, transit systems, factories, ceremonial spaces.
- Materials: translucent ceramic, brushed metal, woven fibers, liquid glass, oxidized copper.
- Time: ancient ritual, present-day intimacy, speculative future, imagined archaeology.
- Scale: miniature worlds, monumental objects, human-scale details, impossible architecture.
- Emotion: anticipation, tenderness, controlled power, playful unease, wonder, solitude.

Do not combine elements randomly. Find a relationship that makes the image feel inevitable once seen.

## Six-concept diversity test

For a batch of six concepts, vary at least four of these dimensions between neighboring concepts:

1. Core premise.
2. Environment.
3. Composition and camera angle.
4. Palette.
5. Lighting.
6. Wardrobe and material language.
7. Mood and narrative.
8. Realism, surrealism, or abstraction.

A set is not diverse if it only changes outfit colors or swaps one background for another.

## Avoid default clichés

Use familiar visual tropes only when the brief calls for them. Otherwise reconsider:
- generic neon city streets;
- random floating geometric shapes;
- predictable luxury hotel interiors;
- ordinary rooftop-at-night scenes;
- generic glowing portals;
- excessive gold trim as a shortcut for luxury;
- decorative fog, sparks, or particles without narrative purpose.

## Premium does not mean overloaded

Premium art direction often comes from restraint:
- one dominant idea;
- a clear focal point;
- controlled color relationships;
- believable materials;
- deliberate lighting;
- precise styling;
- negative space where useful.
